# Experimental findings (Phase 5, rerun with 30 seeds and Holm correction)

All numbers below come from `results/raw/runs.csv` (1,080 runs), aggregated by `scripts/analyze.py` into `results/summary.csv` and `results/stats.txt`; figures are in `paper/figures/`. The unfulfilled-request diagnosis comes from `results/unfulfilled_diagnosis.csv` and `results/unfulfilled_diagnosis_replan.csv` (`scripts/diagnose_unfulfilled.sh`). Reproduce with `scripts/run_experiments.sh` (about 6.5 minutes for 30 seeds) and check determinism with `scripts/check_repro.sh`.

*Change log:* the first version of this file used 10 seeds, for which the smallest attainable Wilcoxon p-value is 0.00195 and no multiple-comparison correction was possible. The grid was rerun with 30 seeds (smallest attainable two-sided p about 1.9e-9) and every p-value is now also reported after Holm-Bonferroni correction over the 93 paired tests. Several headline numbers changed slightly (e.g. large-scale NEAREST lead time 37.4 h to 34.8 h); everything quoted here is the 30-seed result.

## Method in brief
- **Design:** 3 dataset scales (small 40 requests, medium 200, large 1000) x 30 dataset seeds (1-30, each seed regenerates bases, stock, fleet and request stream with `scripts/gen_data.py`) x 4 donor strategies (NEAREST, SURPLUS, BALANCED, RANDOM baseline) x 3 vehicle strategies (SMALLEST, FASTEST, RANDOM baseline) = 1,080 runs.
- **Simulation:** requests arrive at their scenario hour, are approved automatically, planned in priority order every simulated hour, dispatched immediately, and completed at their ETA. A run ends 168 simulated hours after the last arrival (`sim.Simulator`).
- **Metrics:** fulfilment rate (share of requests fully delivered), mean lead time (arrival to full delivery), share of CRITICAL requests delivered within 24 h, fleet utilisation, total distance of delivered trips, why requests remained unfulfilled, wall time and heap in use.
- **Statistics:** means with 95% confidence intervals (t-distribution, n = 30 seeds); paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, pairing by seed (n = 30; n = 29 for CRITICAL-request metrics on `small`, where one seed has no completed CRITICAL request). All 93 tests form one family and are adjusted with the Holm-Bonferroni procedure: 81 are below 0.05 uncorrected and 74 remain below 0.05 after correction (`results/stats.txt`). Where this file says "significant" it means adjusted p < 0.05.

## Findings
**F1 - Donor strategy matters, and BALANCED is best on mean lead time from medium scale upward (RQ1).**
Mean lead time with vehicle strategy FASTEST (`results/summary.csv`, `fig_lead_time_by_source.png`): small - BALANCED 22.9 h, NEAREST 24.3 h, SURPLUS 26.5 h, RANDOM 34.0 h; medium - BALANCED 29.2 h, NEAREST 32.2 h, SURPLUS 36.3 h, RANDOM 48.7 h; large - BALANCED 28.4 h, NEAREST 34.8 h, SURPLUS 40.0 h, RANDOM 55.7 h. BALANCED beats NEAREST by 3.0 h on medium (Holm-adjusted p = 0.012) and 6.3 h on large (p = 1.0e-6); on small the 1.4 h gap is not significant (p = 0.76). For CRITICAL requests NEAREST and BALANCED are indistinguishable at every scale (adjusted p = 1) and both are 7-10 h faster than SURPLUS (adjusted p < 0.001) (`results/stats.txt`).

**F2 - NEAREST minimises transport effort; SURPLUS is the most expensive in distance and slower than NEAREST (RQ1).**
Total distance of delivered trips, large scale: NEAREST 1.188 M km, BALANCED 1.219 M km (+2.6%, adjusted p = 0.003), SURPLUS 1.687 M km (+41.9%), RANDOM 1.958 M km (+64.8%) (`fig_distance_by_source.png`). SURPLUS is also slower than NEAREST: +2.2 h (small, adjusted p = 0.047), +4.2 h (medium, p = 5e-4), +5.3 h (large, p = 1e-6). Taking stock from the base with the most spare units protects small donors but pays for it in distance and time.

**F3 - Fulfilment rate is high everywhere and differs little between sensible strategies (RQ1, partly null).**
Averaged over seeds, every strategy combination fulfils between 95.3% and 99.3% of requests; single runs range from 87.5% to 100% (`results/summary.csv`, `results/raw/runs.csv`, `fig_fulfilment_by_source.png`). On small the donor strategies do not differ (adjusted p >= 0.43). On large BALANCED fulfils 99.3% against 98.3% for NEAREST (adjusted p = 0.001) and 98.8% for SURPLUS (p = 0.003). Why the remaining 1-3% stay open is analysed in F7.

**F4 - FASTEST reduces lead time compared with SMALLEST, at the price of more kilometres and less fleet utilisation (RQ2).**
With donor NEAREST: lead time FASTEST vs SMALLEST -3.8 h (small), -6.7 h (medium), -7.8 h (large), all significant after correction (adjusted p <= 1.6e-4). For CRITICAL requests the gain is -4.1 h (small, adjusted p = 0.055, not significant), -4.4 h (medium, p = 5e-4) and -5.2 h (large, p = 1.7e-7). CRITICAL requests delivered within 24 h: large 83.8% vs 72.5%, medium 78.8% vs 66.0% (adjusted p = 0.001), small 74.1% vs 59.7% (adjusted p = 0.12, not significant) (`fig_vehicle_strategy.png`). Trade-off: FASTEST drives 8.8% (small), 7.0% (medium) and 4.9% (large) more distance, and shows lower utilisation (large 0.305 vs 0.379) because trips are shorter in time. The random vehicle choice is much slower than either policy (large 64.8 h vs 34.8 h with FASTEST) but does not drive significantly more kilometres than FASTEST on small or medium. Which policy is better depends on whether lateness or transport cost matters more.

**F5 - Coordination beats an uncoordinated random baseline by a wide margin, and the gap grows with scale (RQ4).**
NEAREST+FASTEST vs RANDOM+RANDOM: mean lead time 24.3 h vs 46.2 h (small), 32.2 h vs 73.9 h (medium), 34.8 h vs 84.5 h (large); CRITICAL requests within 24 h 74% vs 33% (small), 79% vs 25% (medium), 84% vs 32% (large); total distance -35%, -38%, -42% (`fig_baseline_comparison.png`, `results/stats.txt`; all lead-time, distance and CRITICAL-within-24 h differences are significant after correction, adjusted p <= 5.4e-4). On small the fulfilment rate does **not** differ (0.975 vs 0.971, adjusted p = 1): coordination matters for speed and cost there, not for whether requests are eventually served. On medium (+2.1 percentage points, adjusted p = 5.4e-4) and large (+3.0 pp, adjusted p = 8.4e-5) it also improves fulfilment. The baseline is a deliberately weak random policy, not a real-world competitor, so the size of the gap is an upper bound on practical benefit.

**F6 - The system scales comfortably for this problem size, but time grows faster than linearly (RQ3).**
Median wall time to replay a whole scenario (warm JVM, 12 strategy pairs x 30 seeds per scale): small 3 ms (40 requests), medium 72 ms (200), large 912 ms (1,000 requests over about 1,607 simulated hours), i.e. about 1,100 requests per second on large; heap in use at the end of a run about 61-65 MB (`results/stats.txt`, `fig_scalability.png`). From medium to large the number of requests grows 5x but the time 12.7x, because the hourly planning pass scans a growing backlog and a larger fleet; a simple optimisation (index waiting requests by supply) is left as future work. Interactive commands on the large dataset each finish well under a second.

**F7 - Unfulfilled requests are explained by three mechanisms, mostly the fuel-truck fleet and the donors' reorder floor (diagnosis added after Phase 5).**
Every request still open at the end of a run falls into exactly one of three classes (`sim.Simulator`, columns `stock_limited`, `vehicle_limited`, `in_flight`): (a) *stock-limited* - the units still missing exceed all spare stock at other bases, because donors keep their reorder level (CRITICAL requests may use half of it); (b) *waiting for a vehicle* - stock exists but no suitable vehicle is free; (c) *in flight* - the remaining units are on the road when the 168-hour horizon ends. Shares of the unfulfilled requests over all 360 runs per scale (`results/stats.txt`): small 2.2% of requests unfulfilled - 84% stock-limited, 2% waiting, 13% in flight; medium 1.7% - 22% / 40% / 38%; large 2.1% - 16% / 64% / 20%. A separate diagnosis of NEAREST+FASTEST and RANDOM+RANDOM (30 seeds each, `results/unfulfilled_summary.txt`) shows that on medium and large essentially all requests that are waiting or in flight are **FUEL** requests (e.g. large NEAREST+FASTEST: 252 of 252 waiting, 120 of 120 in flight): the generator gives only about 15% of the fleet to fuel trucks (9 on large), and fuel trucks are slow (40 km/h) so a backlog of fuel requests builds up. 244 further cases on large were first classified "waiting despite an idle vehicle"; all disappear when one more planning pass is run at the horizon end (`results/unfulfilled_diagnosis_replan.csv`), so they are an end-of-horizon artefact (vehicles freed by the last tick were not yet re-planned), not a planning defect. We did **not** test a longer horizon or a larger fuel fleet, so we cannot say how many of the open fuel requests would eventually be served; the 1-3% gap should be read as "within this horizon and fleet".

## Threats to validity
- **Synthetic data:** every input is generated (`docs/dataset.md`); conclusions describe the model, not real operations. The request mix and the engineered shortages favour situations where coordination has work to do.
- **Fleet composition:** the fuel-truck share (about 15%) is a modelling choice and creates the fuel backlog described in F7; results for FUEL requests mainly reflect that choice.
- **Simplified world:** straight-line (haversine) distances, constant speeds, no weather, breakdowns, road networks, stock replenishment or demand that reacts to stock; no delivery failures were injected.
- **Finite horizon:** runs stop 168 h after the last arrival; open requests at that moment count as unfulfilled even if they would be delivered later (F7).
- **Statistics:** 30 seeds with Holm correction over 93 tests; seeds are independent regenerations of the dataset but all come from one generator, so the results generalise across random draws of this generator, not across other kinds of networks. Effect sizes are reported next to p-values because with n = 30 tiny differences can be significant.
- **Baselines are weak by design** (random choice); stronger baselines (e.g. a hand-written dispatcher heuristic) were not tested.
- **Timing and memory** are single-machine, warm-JVM measurements (heap sampled at the end of a run, not the peak) and indicate order of magnitude only.
